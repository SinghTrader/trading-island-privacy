How to Calculate Position Size in Trading

Position sizing determines how much of an asset you trade based on your account balance, risk tolerance and stop-loss distance. Learn the calculation and try it yourself.

What is position sizing in trading?

Position sizing is the process of deciding how many shares, units or contracts to buy or sell in a trade. Rather than choosing a position based only on how much money is available, a risk-based approach starts with the amount a trader is prepared to lose.

For example, two traders may both have a $10,000 account, but one may purchase 50 shares while the other purchases 200 shares. Their exposure and potential losses can be very different, even when they trade the same instrument.

A position size should account for the distance between the planned entry price and the stop loss, the value of each price movement and the characteristics of the instrument being traded.

Why position sizing matters

Every trading strategy experiences uncertainty. Even a setup that has performed well historically can produce consecutive losing trades. Position sizing helps define the amount at risk before entering a position.

Consider a $10,000 trading account. A loss of 10% reduces the balance to $9,000. A loss of 1% reduces it to $9,900. These are very different outcomes from a single trade.

Example: five consecutive losing trades

Starting account balance: $10,000

Risking 1% of the remaining balance per trade results in approximately $9,509.90 after five full-risk losses, excluding costs.

Risking 5% of the remaining balance per trade results in approximately $7,737.81.

This illustrates how position sizing influences drawdowns. It does not mean that either percentage is appropriate for every trader.

How to calculate position size

For a simple stock trade where the account and stock prices use the same currency, the calculation involves three main inputs:

  • Account balance: the amount in the trading account.
  • Risk percentage: the planned fraction of the account at risk.
  • Stop-loss distance: the difference between entry and stop-loss prices.
Risk amount = Account balance × (Risk percentage ÷ 100)

Position size = Risk amount ÷ |Entry price − Stop-loss price|

The result is the theoretical number of shares or units for instruments where a one-unit price movement changes the value of one held unit by one unit of the account currency.

For derivatives, forex pairs and instruments with contract multipliers, the calculation also needs the correct contract value and potentially a currency conversion.

Important: A stop-loss order does not guarantee execution at its specified price. Gaps, slippage, fees and other costs can make realized losses greater than the amount calculated.

Position sizing example

Suppose a trader has a $10,000 account and plans to risk 1% on a stock trade.

Example long trade

Account balance: $10,000
Risk percentage: 1%
Entry price: $100
Stop loss: $98

Risk budget = $10,000 × 0.01 = $100
Risk per share = $100 − $98 = $2
Position size = $100 ÷ $2 = 50 shares

Estimated position size: 50 shares

The position has a notional value of $5,000 at the entry price. If all 50 shares are sold at $98, the price-based loss is $100 before fees and other execution costs.

Notice that risking $100 does not mean buying only $100 worth of shares. The amount at risk depends on the price movement to the planned exit, not just the total purchase value.

Position sizing across different markets

The underlying risk principle is similar across markets, but the units and contract specifications can differ substantially.

Market Position unit Additional consideration
Stocks Shares Share price and available buying power
Forex Currency units or lots Pip value, lot size and currency conversion
Futures Contracts Tick size, tick value and contract multiplier
CFDs Broker-defined contracts Contract size, financing and margin requirements
Options Option contracts Premium, multiplier and nonlinear price behavior

For example, a futures contract may represent many units of an underlying asset. A $1 movement in the quoted price may therefore create a profit or loss substantially larger than $1.

Similarly, forex traders need to understand the value of a pip for their particular position size and account currency.

Position sizing and stop-loss placement

Position sizing and stop-loss placement are connected, but they serve different purposes. The stop-loss level defines where a trade is intended to be exited if the market moves against the position.

The position size determines the number of units that can be traded within the selected risk budget.

A wider stop-loss distance generally requires a smaller position size to maintain the same planned monetary risk. A tighter stop-loss distance generally permits a larger position size under the same calculation.

Same risk budget, different stop distances

Risk budget: $100

$1 stop distance → 100 shares
$2 stop distance → 50 shares
$4 stop distance → 25 shares

However, a trader should not move a stop loss closer to the entry merely to increase position size. Stop placement should reflect the trade setup and market conditions.

Common position sizing mistakes

  • Using the same number of shares for every trade. Different prices and stop-loss distances can create very different levels of risk.
  • Confusing position value with money at risk. A $5,000 position does not necessarily mean that $5,000 is the planned stop-loss risk.
  • Ignoring leverage. Leverage changes the capital required to hold a position but does not eliminate market exposure or potential losses.
  • Ignoring fees and slippage. Actual losses may exceed the theoretical price-based estimate.
  • Using the wrong contract multiplier. A calculation suitable for shares may be incorrect for futures, CFDs or options.
  • Increasing risk after losing trades. Emotional changes to position sizing can produce losses inconsistent with a trading plan.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 1% rule in trading?

The 1% rule is a risk-management guideline under which a trader plans to risk no more than 1% of their account on a single trade. It is not a universal requirement or a guarantee against larger realized losses.

How do I calculate position size with a stop loss?

First calculate the monetary risk budget. Then divide that budget by the price difference between entry and stop loss, adjusted for the instrument's unit value and currency.

Does leverage change my position size calculation?

Leverage affects margin and buying power. The monetary exposure to price movement still depends on the position size and instrument specifications. Leverage can amplify losses relative to deposited capital.

Can I use this calculator for forex?

Not directly. Forex lot sizing requires the correct pip value, lot specifications and sometimes currency conversion. This calculator assumes simple unit-priced assets in the account currency.

Can I lose more than the calculated risk?

Yes. Price gaps, slippage, transaction costs and other market conditions may result in a loss greater than the planned amount.
