Risk-to-Reward Ratio in Trading: How to Calculate It

Risk-to-reward compares how much you plan to risk on a trade with how much you aim to gain. Here is how the calculation works — and what the number does not tell you.

What is risk-to-reward ratio?

A risk-to-reward ratio compares the potential loss you accept if a trade reaches its stop loss with the potential gain if it reaches its target. It is a planning metric, not a guarantee that either price will be reached.

If you risk €100 to target a €200 gain, the potential reward is twice the potential risk. Traders commonly describe this as a 1:2 risk-to-reward ratio.

How do you calculate risk-to-reward?

Determine the distance between your entry and stop loss, then the distance between your entry and profit target. Divide potential reward by potential risk.

Reward-to-risk multiple = Potential reward ÷ Potential risk

A result of 2 corresponds to 1:2; a result of 3 corresponds to 1:3.

Risk-to-reward example

Example long trade

Entry: $100
Stop loss: $98
Target: $106

Risk per unit = $2
Potential reward per unit = $6

$6 ÷ $2 = 3 → 1:3 risk-to-reward

Risk-to-reward and win rate work together

A larger potential reward does not automatically make a strategy better. Risk-to-reward needs to be considered together with how often trades win, actual execution, costs and whether planned targets and stops are realistically reached.

Expectancy = (Win rate × Average win) − (Loss rate × Average loss)

Common risk-to-reward mistakes

  • Choosing a target only to create an attractive ratio. A large target is not useful merely because the number looks good.
  • Ignoring win rate. R:R describes payoff size, not how often a setup succeeds.
  • Confusing planned R:R with realized R:R. Early exits, moved stops or slippage can change the actual result.
  • Ignoring trading costs. Fees, spreads and slippage can reduce realized performance.
  • Judging a strategy from only a few trades. Small samples can be noisy.

Should every trade have the same risk-to-reward?

Not necessarily. Different setups, market structures and exit methods can produce different potential ratios. The useful comparison is between your planned trade logic and your actual results over a meaningful set of trades.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 1:2 risk-to-reward ratio good?

A 1:2 ratio means planned reward is twice planned risk. Whether that produces positive results depends on win rate, actual average wins and losses, costs and execution.

What does 1:3 risk-to-reward mean?

It means three units of potential reward for each one unit of potential risk. Risking €100 with a €300 target is a simple example.

Can a strategy be profitable with a low win rate?

Mathematically, sufficiently large average wins relative to average losses can offset a lower win rate. Actual profitability still depends on the complete distribution of results and costs.
